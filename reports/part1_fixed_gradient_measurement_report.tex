\begin{abstract}
This report defines and compares three measurement strategies for the generator-selection step in gradient-based ADAPT-VQE (adaptive derivative-assembled pseudo-Trotter variational quantum eigensolver): \textbf{M1: FC-UG}, fully commuting (FC) universal groups for static all-gradient estimation; \textbf{M2: BAI-FC-IG}, best-arm identification (BAI) using FC individual-gradient measurements; and \textbf{M3: BAI-FC-UG}, best-arm identification using fixed parent FC universal groups. A fourth construction, a \emph{no-sharing baseline} that uses neither mechanism, completes the two-by-two the three methods span and makes each mechanism separately attributable.

Two classes of number are reported, and they are kept apart throughout. \emph{Planning bounds} (Secs.~\ref{sec:completed}--\ref{sec:baseline}) are oracle diagnostics: exact gradients and exact fragment variances are used to compute the shots each method would need, and the elimination rule reads exact values, so it cannot misfire. A \emph{finite-shot surrogate} (Sec.~\ref{sec:finite-shot}) replaces the exact gradients with Gaussian estimates built from a finite number of shots and runs the same elimination rule on those. The surrogate samples estimators, not individual measurement outcomes, so it is not a shot-level simulation of a quantum device; the distinction and its consequences are set out in Sec.~\ref{sec:assumptions}.

\textbf{Results.} On the planning bounds, M3 is cheaper than M1 in all nine cases, by factors of $1.1$ to $192$, and cheaper than M2 in eight of nine. The M2-versus-M3 comparison is \emph{unresolved} on every H$_2$O row: the ratio moves over $0.74$ to $1.30$ across arbitrary choices of grouping routine, which spans unity. Against the no-sharing baseline, elimination alone is worth $1.3$ to $253$ and sharing alone only $1.1$ to $3.4$, and the two mechanisms are substitutes rather than complements: the interaction term is below unity in all nine cases. Under the finite-shot surrogate both best-arm methods cost more than their bounds, M2 by $2.2$ to $3.2$ and M3 by $1.4$ to $3.0$; an unseparated part of that is discretisation of the radius schedule rather than noise, and no mechanism for the difference between the two methods is established. Because M1 realises its bound exactly, the comparisons against M1 reorder under finite shots and M3 loses on two of seven rows. Every trial of the main finite-shot table returned the correct generator, which at 200 trials bounds the error rate only below $1.9\%$; the stated family-wise failure probability does not control that rate.

\textbf{Scope.} Every number is a single-ADAPT-step diagnostic for one fixed state, and counts shots only: circuit depth, the number of distinct measurement settings, and state-preparation cost are excluded (Sec.~\ref{sec:assumptions}). Part III symmetry filtering and tapering are not included in the Part I tables; Part III supplies a smaller input problem to the same measurement methods. The revision history of this document, the naming convention for code and files, and the implementation status are in Apps.~\ref{app:revision}--\ref{app:status}.
\end{abstract}

\section{Problem statement}
\label{sec:problem}

Given a molecular Hamiltonian $H$, a current ADAPT-VQE state $\ket{\psi}$, and a unitary coupled-cluster singles-and-doubles (UCCSD) generator pool $\{G_i\}$, estimate the measurement cost required to identify a generator with the largest absolute gradient
\begin{equation}
  i^\star = \argmax_i |g_i|,
  \qquad
  g_i = \bra{\psi}\comm{H}{G_i}\ket{\psi} .
  \label{eq:gradient}
\end{equation}
The scientific question is whether using winner-identification statistics and shared fully commuting measurement contexts reduces the gradient-selection measurement cost relative to static all-gradient estimation.

\textbf{The answer, in one sentence.} Mostly yes: on oracle planning bounds at family-wise $\delta=0.05$, combining both mechanisms (M3) costs $1.1$ to $192$ times less than static all-gradient estimation (M1) across the nine cases studied --- but the change of statistical target carries the saving in seven of the nine, shared contexts being worth only $1.1$ to $3.4$ alone; the advantage shrinks under finite shots and reverses on two rows; and the comparison between the two best-arm variants is unresolved on the largest system.

Here $g_i$ is the derivative of the energy with respect to the variational parameter of generator $G_i$ at $\theta_i=0$, in Hartree per radian; $G_i$ is anti-Hermitian, so $g_i$ is real. The generator pool and its labelling convention are fixed in Sec.~\ref{sec:computational}.

\section{Object lineage}

The qubit Hamiltonian is
\begin{equation}
  H=\sum_p h_p P_p,
\end{equation}
where $P_p$ are Pauli products after Jordan--Wigner mapping. For each generator $G_i$, define the gradient operator
\begin{equation}
  C_i = \comm{H}{G_i} .
\end{equation}
The ADAPT gradient is the expectation value of this operator,
\begin{equation}
  g_i=\expect{C_i}_{\psi}.
\end{equation}
Expanding all gradient operators in one shared Pauli-coordinate system gives
\begin{equation}
  C_i=\sum_{\ell} A_{i\ell}R_{\ell},
  \qquad
  \mu_\ell=\expect{R_\ell}_{\psi},
  \qquad
  \bm g = A\bm\mu .
  \label{eq:gAmu}
\end{equation}
The parent universal Pauli support at this ADAPT step is
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal B_0=\bigcup_i \supp(C_i).
  \label{eq:B0}
\end{equation}
M1 and M3 use $\mathcal B_0$ as a shared measurement object. M2 does not; it groups each $C_i$ separately.

\section{The three algorithms}
\label{sec:algorithms}

\subsection{M1: FC-UG}

M1 estimates the full gradient vector. It forms one global FC grouping of the parent support,
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal B_0 \longrightarrow \mathcal M_1,\ldots,\mathcal M_K,
\end{equation}
where each $\mathcal M_\alpha$ is a fully commuting measurement context. Measurements of these contexts estimate the shared Pauli expectations $\mu_\ell$ and reconstruct every gradient using Eq.~\eqref{eq:gAmu}.

The implementation uses deterministic greedy FC grouping of $\mathcal B_0$ (Sec.~\ref{sec:computational}). This is a global fully commuting all-gradient baseline in the spirit of the grouping strategies of Refs.~\cite{Anastasiou2023ReallyMeasure,Shkolnikov2023Avoiding}, in that it shares measurement contexts across the whole ADAPT gradient pool; it is not claimed to reproduce the specialised constructions of either. [AUTHOR: state what M2 and M3 add beyond Refs.~\cite{Huang2025QuantumGambling,Anastasiou2023ReallyMeasure}, and cite Refs.~\cite{Grimsley2019ADAPT,Rossi2026RSe} where they are used.]

\subsection{M2: BAI-FC-IG}

M2 changes the statistical target from full-vector estimation to winner identification. Each candidate gradient is an arm in a best-arm-identification problem. Its own commutator support is grouped separately,
\begin{equation}
  \supp(C_i) \longrightarrow \mathcal M_{i,1},\ldots,\mathcal M_{i,K_i} .
\end{equation}
Throughout, $t$ indexes the BAI round and $r$ denotes a confidence radius; the two are never the same symbol. BAI maintains confidence intervals for active gradients and eliminates a generator $i$ at round $t$ if
\begin{equation}
  |\widehat g_i|+r_i < \max_{j\in\mathcal A_t}\left(|\widehat g_j|-r_j\right),
  \label{eq:bai-elim}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal A_t$ is the active generator set and $r_i=z_\delta\,\epsilon_i$ is the current confidence radius of arm $i$, with $\epsilon_i$ its one-standard-error target and $z_\delta$ the confidence factor of Eq.~\eqref{eq:zdelta}. All methods in this report use a single common radius $r_i=r$ across active arms; the rule is written with per-arm radii because the covariance-aware variant of Sec.~\ref{sec:correlation} does not. M2 does not exploit universal cross-gradient sharing.

\textbf{Implementation status.} M2 is now implemented as its own module. Each run writes the individual-gradient FC grouping $\mathcal M_{i,k}$ for every generator, the oracle fragment standard deviation of every fragment, the per-arm shot cost, and the full BAI elimination history with confidence radii and active sets. The M2 column is therefore auditable; it remains an oracle planning diagnostic rather than a finite-shot result.

\subsection{M3: BAI-FC-UG}

M3 combines the mechanisms of M1 and M2. It starts with the same fixed parent universal FC contexts as M1,
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal M_1,\ldots,\mathcal M_K,
\end{equation}
but BAI eliminates generator candidates,
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal A_0 \supset \mathcal A_1 \supset \mathcal A_2 \supset \cdots .
\end{equation}
At BAI round $t$, the active Pauli support is
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal B_t=\bigcup_{i\in\mathcal A_t}\supp(C_i),
  \qquad
  \mathcal B_t\subseteq\mathcal B_0 .
\end{equation}
The active measurement contexts are restricted parent contexts,
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal M_\alpha^{(t)}=\mathcal M_\alpha\cap\mathcal B_t .
\end{equation}
The parent contexts are not rebuilt during BAI. This nested construction keeps previous measurements compatible while allowing the algorithm to stop sampling information that only affects eliminated generators.

\textbf{How M3's cost is aggregated.} Shots taken in a context are reusable, so a context is charged once, at the largest number of shots any round ever demanded of it. Writing $n_\alpha^{(t)}$ for the allocation of Eq.~\eqref{eq:allocation} solved over the active set $\mathcal A_t$ at the radius of round $t$, the reported M3 cost is
\begin{equation}
  M_{\mathrm{M3}}=\sum_{\alpha}\max_{t} n_\alpha^{(t)} .
  \label{eq:m3-aggregate}
\end{equation}
Each round's allocation is solved independently, without reference to shots already committed; the maximum is taken afterwards. This is the accounting a real run would get for free by keeping its data, and it is why the trajectory is fully determined by the finitely many rounds at which the active set changes.

\section{Oracle shot proxy}
\label{sec:proxy}

All numbers in Secs.~\ref{sec:completed}--\ref{sec:baseline} are oracle planning estimates. The state $\ket{\psi}$ is known exactly in the simulation, so the exact gradient values and exact fragment variances are available. No empirical finite-shot variance learning is included. \emph{Oracle} and \emph{noiseless} are used here as synonyms for this one property: the planner is given the exact $g_i$ and $\sigma_{i\alpha}$. They do not refer to a noiseless quantum device, and they say nothing about state-preparation error, which is excluded from the model altogether (Sec.~\ref{sec:assumptions}).

\textbf{Sorted order and the gap.} Order the pool by decreasing absolute gradient, $|g_{(1)}|\ge|g_{(2)}|\ge\cdots$, so that $(1)$ denotes the winner and $(2)$ the runner-up. The parenthesised index is the rank, never a generator index. The top gap and the deficit of generator $i$ are
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{gap}=|g_{(1)}|-|g_{(2)}|,
  \qquad
  \Delta_i=|g_{(1)}|-|g_i| \ \ge 0 ,
  \label{eq:gap}
\end{equation}
so $\Delta_{(2)}=\mathrm{gap}$ and $\Delta_{(1)}=0$.

\textbf{Shots and context-shots.} One \emph{shot} is one state preparation followed by one measurement of one measurement context: a single circuit execution. A \emph{context-shot} is the same thing counted against the context it was spent in; the two terms are interchangeable in this report, and every cost quoted is a total over contexts, $\sum_\alpha n_\alpha$. The metric counts shots only. It does not count the depth of the diagonalising circuits, the number of \emph{distinct} measurement settings a method requires, or any classical post-processing; Sec.~\ref{sec:assumptions} gives the distinct-setting counts, which differ between the methods by a factor of several and would have to be weighed separately in a device-level comparison.

For one gradient operator written as a sum of independently measured FC fragments,
\begin{equation}
  C_i = \sum_{\alpha=1}^{K_i} F_{i\alpha},
\end{equation}
with oracle fragment variances
\begin{equation}
  \sigma^2_{i\alpha}=\Var_\psi(F_{i\alpha})
  =\expect{F_{i\alpha}^2}_\psi-\expect{F_{i\alpha}}_\psi^2,
\end{equation}
the optimal independent-fragment allocation gives the approximate total number of shots needed to estimate $g_i$ with one-standard-error target $\epsilon_i$ as
\begin{equation}
  M_i(\epsilon_i)\approx
  \frac{\left(\sum_{\alpha=1}^{K_i}\sigma_{i\alpha}\right)^2}{\epsilon_i^2} .
  \label{eq:single-gradient-shot}
\end{equation}
\textbf{Precision target and confidence radius.} $\epsilon_i$ is a one-standard-error target on $\widehat g_i$; a \emph{confidence radius} is the half-width of the corresponding interval,
\begin{equation}
  r_i=z_\delta\,\epsilon_i ,
  \label{eq:radius}
\end{equation}
so a method asked for radius $r$ must reach $\epsilon=r/z_\delta$, and $\epsilon_i$ in Eq.~\eqref{eq:single-gradient-shot} is replaced by $r_i/z_\delta$. The confidence factor is the two-sided normal quantile with a Bonferroni correction over the whole pool,
\begin{equation}
  z_\delta=\Phi^{-1}\!\left(1-\frac{\delta}{2N}\right),
  \qquad N=|\{G_i\}| ,
  \label{eq:zdelta}
\end{equation}
with $N$ the \emph{full} pool size --- $26$ for H$_4$, $92$ for LiH, $140$ for H$_2$O --- not the number of non-zero gradients and not the current active set. No correction is taken over rounds. At $\delta=0.05$ this gives $z_\delta=3.10$ for H$_4$, $3.46$ for LiH and $3.57$ for H$_2$O. The same $z_\delta$ is applied to all four constructions, so it cancels from every planning-bound ratio (Sec.~\ref{sec:sensitivity}). [AUTHOR: justify $\delta=0.05$ and the Bonferroni correction.] Sec.~\ref{sec:calibration} shows that this construction does \emph{not} control the realised selection-error rate, so it should be read as a convention fixed identically across the methods rather than as a guarantee. For BAI the tolerance for an arm is set by the current separation between its estimated absolute gradient and the best surviving one.

Eq.~\eqref{eq:single-gradient-shot} is applied separately inside each gradient arm, because M2 measures each arm in its own contexts. M1 and M3 instead share contexts between generators, so the allocation over contexts is not separable and is obtained by solving the convex programme
\begin{equation}
  \min_{n_\alpha \ge 0} \ \sum_{\alpha} n_\alpha
  \qquad\text{subject to}\qquad
  \Var(\widehat g_i)=\sum_{\alpha}\frac{\sigma_{i\alpha}^{2}}{n_\alpha}\le\epsilon^{2}
  \quad\text{for every active } i ,
  \label{eq:allocation}
\end{equation}
where $n_\alpha$ is the number of shots taken in context $\alpha$ and $\Var(\widehat g_i)=\sum_\alpha\sigma_{i\alpha}^2/n_\alpha$ is the \emph{reconstruction variance} of gradient $i$ under that allocation: the variance of the estimate obtained by reading $\widehat g_i$ off the shared contexts through Eq.~\eqref{eq:gAmu}. The quantity $\sum_\alpha\sigma_{i\alpha}^2$ that appears repeatedly below is its value at one shot per context, and serves as a scale-free measure of how expensive generator $i$ is to reconstruct from the parent grouping. Its stationarity condition is $n_\alpha=\sqrt{\sum_i\lambda_i\sigma_{i\alpha}^{2}}$ for multipliers $\lambda_i\ge0$, which is solved by a damped multiplicative fixed-point iteration and then rescaled so that the tightest constraint holds exactly. The iteration uses multiplicative damping $0.5$, at most $500$ iterations, and stops when the least-loaded constraint is within $10^{-9}$ of the tightest; the final rescaling makes the returned allocation feasible whatever the iteration converged to, so the stopping rule affects how close to optimal the answer is and never its validity. For a single gradient it reduces exactly to Eq.~\eqref{eq:single-gradient-shot}, and it agrees with a general-purpose convex solver on the small instances tested (Sec.~\ref{sec:tests}). A previous envelope heuristic, which spread shots in proportion to $\max_i\sigma_{i\alpha}$, overestimated the M1 and M3 costs by up to a factor of eight and inverted the M2/M3 comparison; it has been replaced.

\textbf{Shot counts are not rounded.} $n_\alpha$ is left real-valued and no per-context minimum is imposed; the per-arm closed form of Eq.~\eqref{eq:m2-exact} used by M2 and the baseline is rounded up to an integer per arm. Imposing a floor of one shot per context would add at most $K$ shots to a total, which is at most $0.2\%$ of any entry in Table~\ref{tab:completed} --- the worst case is the smallest total, M3 on H$_4$ side $2.0$ CISD, where $53$ contexts sit under $33{,}432$ shots. It would change the digits quoted but not any ratio.

The BAI methods are evaluated in the schedule-free limit. As the common confidence radius $r$ decreases continuously, generator $i$ leaves the active set the moment $2r$ falls below its absolute-gradient deficit
$\Delta_i$ of Eq.~\eqref{eq:gap},
so it is resolved to exactly $\Delta_i/2$; the leader survives to $\mathrm{gap}/2$. Because measurements are reusable, an arm that leaves at radius $r$ has paid the cost of being resolved to $r$ and nothing more, and a shared context has paid the largest requirement it ever saw. The active set changes only at the finitely many distinct values of $\Delta_i$, so the whole trajectory is determined by evaluating the allocation at those radii. For M2 this collapses to the closed form of Eq.~\eqref{eq:m2-exact}. This replaces the geometric radius schedule of the previous revision, whose shrink factor was a free parameter and which, being coarse, overshot the elimination points; Sec.~\ref{sec:sensitivity} quantifies the difference.

Because these formulae depend on the exact grouping and fragment variances, every production run saves the FC groups, the fragment variances and the BAI histories used to generate each table entry.

\subsection{Generator pool, conventions and labels}

Spin orbitals are interleaved: spatial orbital $p$ gives spin orbitals $2p$ ($\alpha$) and $2p+1$ ($\beta$), zero-based. The computational basis is indexed big-endian, matching the sparse-operator convention of the linear-algebra backend. The Jordan--Wigner transform is used throughout.

The generator universe is the standard spin-orbital UCCSD pool. Each generator is anti-Hermitian,
\begin{equation}
  G_i=\mathcal{JW}\!\left(\tau_i-\tau_i^\dagger\right),
  \qquad
  \tau_{i}^{\text{S}}=a_a^\dagger a_i,
  \qquad
  \tau_{i}^{\text{D}}=a_a^\dagger a_b^\dagger a_j a_i ,
\end{equation}
so that $g_i=\expect{\comm{H}{G_i}}_\psi$ of Eq.~\eqref{eq:gradient} is real and equals $\partial E/\partial\theta_i$ at $\theta_i=0$ for the parameterisation $e^{\theta_i G_i}$. Singles run over occupied--virtual pairs of the same spin; doubles conserve $N_\alpha$ and $N_\beta$ separately. The pool therefore conserves electron number and $S_z$ but not generally $S^2$. Ordering is deterministic: all singles in ascending $(i,a)$, then all doubles in ascending $(i,j,a,b)$. The labels used in the tables are exactly this: \texttt{S i -> a} for a single and \texttt{D i,j -> a,b} for a double, with zero-based interleaved spin-orbital indices. The resulting pool sizes are $26$, $92$ and $140$ for H$_4$, LiH and H$_2$O. [AUTHOR: justify this pool against qubit or spin-adapted alternatives.]

\subsection{Fully commuting grouping routine}

FC group counts are routine-dependent, and two rows are comparable only if produced by the same deterministic routine with the same Pauli ordering, tie-breaking rule and seed. Every row in the main tables uses one routine: deterministic greedy first-fit grouping, with Pauli strings sorted by decreasing weight (the number of non-identity factors) and ties broken by ascending lexicographic order of the string read left to right from qubit $0$, under the letter order $\mathrm{I}<\mathrm{X}<\mathrm{Y}<\mathrm{Z}$, with each candidate joining the lowest-numbered existing group that accepts it and opening a new group otherwise. No random seed is used. Sec.~\ref{sec:routines} varies this convention deliberately and reports the spread. The counts differ from the previous revision, whose H$_4$ and H$_2$O rows used a different partitioning routine and were not comparable with the LiH row. [AUTHOR: justify fully commuting grouping against qubit-wise commuting and other schemes.]

\subsection{Validation gate}

Every case is checked before any gradient is computed: the Jordan--Wigner (JW) Hamiltonian must reproduce the PySCF restricted Hartree--Fock (RHF) energy on the Hartree--Fock (HF) determinant and the PySCF full configuration interaction (FCI) energy as its lowest eigenvalue in the $(N_\alpha,N_\beta)$ sector, both to $10^{-8}$ Ha. All nine cases pass, with a maximum observed error of $4\times10^{-15}$ Ha. A case that fails the gate aborts rather than producing numbers.

\subsection{Non-zero gradient threshold}

Gradients that vanish by symmetry appear at the $10^{-8}$ numerical noise floor of the integral and SCF pipeline, while the smallest physically non-zero gradient in these cases is of order $10^{-3}$. The counts below use a threshold of $10^{-6}$, which lies on the plateau between the two. The threshold is not innocuous at tighter values: for LiH the count is 26 at $10^{-6}$ and 34 at $10^{-9}$, and every run records the count at $10^{-4}$, $10^{-6}$, $10^{-8}$ and $10^{-9}$ so the choice stays visible. \textbf{The threshold is used for reporting only.} No method filters the pool by it: all four constructions carry every one of the $N$ generators as an arm, and $N$ in Eq.~\eqref{eq:zdelta} is the full pool size. The one place the distinction is used analytically is the diagnostic in Sec.~\ref{sec:m1-cost}, which asks what M1 would cost if it were given the non-zero set for free.

\section{What the model assumes and excludes}
\label{sec:assumptions}

\textbf{Statistical assumptions, in one place.} (i) Estimators are Gaussian; the normal limit is assumed, not tested against shot-level sampling of measurement outcomes. (ii) Distinct measurement contexts are sampled independently. (iii) Within a context, the planning bounds and the main finite-shot tables treat different generators' fragments as independent; this is exact for M2 and the baseline, which give every generator its own shots, and an approximation for M1 and M3. Sec.~\ref{sec:correlation} removes it and reports what changes. (iv) State preparation is exact and noiseless, and there is no gate, readout or decoherence error anywhere in the model. (v) The union bound behind Eq.~\eqref{eq:zdelta} is taken over the $N$ pool members only; it is not taken over the sequence of elimination rounds, which in the exact limit is a continuum. (vi) Fragment variances are the exact $\Var_\psi(F_{i\alpha})$, so no cost is charged for learning them.

\textbf{``System size'' means the pool.} The phrase is used below to order the
four systems, and the orderings induced by the plausible measures do not
coincide, so it is fixed here: \emph{system size} means the generator pool size
$N$, which orders the systems H$_4$ $(26)$ $<$ LiH $(92)$ $<$ H$_2$O $(140)$. On
these four systems that ordering happens to agree with qubit count $(8,12,14)$ and
with $|\mathcal B_0|$ $(1{,}276;\ 30{,}196;\ 81{,}566)$, but not with electron
count, where LiH and H$_2$O both exceed H$_4$ in the other direction. Because only
four systems are present and geometry and state vary with them, no claim in this
report about a trend with system size is established as such; the phrase is a
label for the ordering, not a scaling result.

\textbf{What the cost metric leaves out.} Every number counts shots. It does not count the depth or two-qubit gate count of the circuit that diagonalises a context, the cost of compiling or calibrating distinct measurement settings, or classical post-processing. The number of distinct settings differs sharply between the methods, and in the direction that disfavours the individually grouped ones.

\textbf{Why the two H$_2$O geometries have the same support size but different context counts.} The grouping routine is deterministic in the set of Pauli strings alone, so equal supports would have to give equal counts; $2{,}366$ against $2{,}356$ therefore means the supports are not equal. They are not. Both contain $81{,}566$ products, and $\sum_i|\supp(C_i)|$ is $314{,}720$ in both, but their intersection is only $78{,}113$: each geometry has $3{,}453$ Pauli products the other lacks, a symmetric difference of $6{,}906$, or $8.5\%$ of each. The differing products are not numerical dust --- their largest commutator coefficients reach $0.09$ and $0.11$, and their medians, $2.6\times10^{-3}$ and $3.7\times10^{-3}$, are within a factor of two of the median over the shared products.

The cause is an orbital reordering. Both geometries have the same point group and the same multiset of orbital symmetries, but the Hartree--Fock orbital energies cross between them: the canonical order is $(A_1,A_1,B_2,A_1,B_1,A_1,B_2)$ at $R(\mathrm{OH})=0.9572$~\si{\angstrom} and $(A_1,A_1,B_1,B_2,A_1,A_1,B_2)$ at $1.75$~\si{\angstrom}. The symmetry-allowed integrals are the same in number, which is why $|\mathcal B_0|$ and $\sum_i|\supp(C_i)|$ agree exactly, but they sit at different orbital indices, so they map to different Pauli strings under Jordan--Wigner. The same reordering is why the top generator is \texttt{D 4,5 $\to$ 12,13} at equilibrium and \texttt{D 6,7 $\to$ 12,13} when stretched. The two geometries are therefore comparable as physics but their Pauli-level structures are not identical, and a difference of ten contexts between them carries no information.

\section{Complete three-method comparison}

\subsection{Where the cost actually sits: breadth and depth}
\label{sec:breadth-depth}

The shot cost of a BAI method is not concentrated in the final elimination, as a
naive reading of the $1/r^2$ scaling suggests. It splits into two terms, and
which one dominates changes with the system.

\textbf{One definition, applied the same way to both methods.} Let
$r_0=\tfrac12\max_i\Delta_i$ be the coarsest radius of the trajectory, the radius
at which the first generator is eliminated and at which every generator is still
active. \emph{Breadth} is the cost of that first sweep,
\begin{equation}
  M^{\text{breadth}} = \text{cost of resolving every generator to } r_0 ,
  \qquad
  M^{\text{depth}} = M^{\text{total}} - M^{\text{breadth}} ,
  \label{eq:breadth-depth}
\end{equation}
and the \emph{breadth share} is $M^{\text{breadth}}/M^{\text{total}}$. For M2 the
cost is separable, so $M^{\text{breadth}}=\sum_i (z_\delta S_i^{\text{own}}/r_0)^2$,
writing $S_i^{\text{own}}=\sum_{\alpha}\sigma_{i\alpha}$ over generator $i$'s own
contexts and $S_i^{\text{par}}$ for the same sum over the parent contexts. For M3 it is
the total of the allocation at the first breakpoint, before any elimination.
Because shots are reusable, breadth is a cost neither method can avoid: it is the
price of learning which generators are worth pursuing.

The two terms reward sharing very differently. In the breadth term a shared
context serves the entire pool, 92 or 140 generators at once, and M3's advantage
over M2 is correspondingly large. In the depth term it serves only the generators
that survive to the final radius. M3's breadth share is below M2's in every one of
the nine rows, which is what sharing is supposed to do: it makes the coarse sweep
cheaper relative to the rest.

\subsection{What decides the depth term, and why the M3 advantage may not survive}
\label{sec:fragmentation}

Two quantities decide the depth term. The first is the \emph{fragmentation penalty}. Generator $i$'s own FC grouping is $\mathcal M_{i,1},\ldots,\mathcal M_{i,K_i}$ as in Sec.~\ref{sec:algorithms}; write $F_{i\alpha}$ for the part of $C_i$ that lands in parent context $\mathcal M_\alpha$, so that the parent grouping splits $C_i$ into $K_i^{\text{parent}}$ pieces instead of $K_i^{\text{own}}\equiv K_i$. Then
\begin{equation}
  f_i=\left(\frac{\sum_{\alpha}\sigma_{i\alpha}\ \text{over the parent contexts}}
                 {\sum_{k}\sigma_{i k}\ \text{over its own contexts } \mathcal M_{i,k}}\right)^{2}
  ,
  \label{eq:fragmentation}
\end{equation}
is the factor by which the shared grouping makes that one gradient more expensive to resolve on its own. It is above unity in every case measured here, and the reason is that Eq.~\eqref{eq:single-gradient-shot} depends on $\left(\sum_\alpha\sigma_{i\alpha}\right)^2$ and not on $\sum_\alpha\sigma_{i\alpha}^2$, so splitting an operator into more pieces increases the sum of the pieces' standard deviations. This is not a theorem: the parent grouping is not a refinement of the generator's own grouping --- a parent context can merge Paulis that the own grouping separates --- so $f_i\ge1$ is an observation, supported by $K^{\text{parent}}$ exceeding $K^{\text{own}}$ by factors of $2$ to $16$ in Table~\ref{tab:frag}. The second quantity is
\begin{equation}
  |\mathcal A|=\left|\left\{i:\Delta_i\le\mathrm{gap}\right\}\right| ,
\end{equation}
the number of generators still active at the final radius $\mathrm{gap}/2$. By construction $|\mathcal A|\ge2$, the leader and the runner-up.

In Table~\ref{tab:frag}, $K^{\text{own}}$ is the number of groups in the winner's own FC grouping and depends only on $\supp(C_i)$, hence not on the state. $K^{\text{parent}}$ is the number of parent contexts in which the winner has a \emph{non-zero} fragment standard deviation. It therefore does depend on the state, which is why it differs between the HF and CISD rows of H$_2$O ($858$ against $887$, and $875$ against $899$) even though the winner and its support are the same operator: a fragment whose variance vanishes in one state carries information in the other. Contexts where $\sigma_{i\alpha}=0$ contribute nothing to Eq.~\eqref{eq:single-gradient-shot} and are not counted.

\textbf{This is a hypothesis, not a demonstration.} $f$ is not monotone in system size --- LiH, the smaller system, gives $3.49$ against $2.54$ for H$_2$O at equilibrium --- four systems cannot separate size from geometry and state, and $f$ grows far more slowly than the raw context count ($3.6$ against $17.5$ on H$_2$O stretch), because most parent contexts hold a negligible sliver of any one gradient. The mechanism is real but weak, and nothing here fixes where or whether a crossover occurs. \subsection{An alternative: rebuild the grouping once the field narrows}

Staged regrouping does beat both methods, but only by $3$ to $11\%$, and the best switch point is not consistent: early on H$_4$, late on H$_2$O, and never on LiH, where the procedure degenerates to M3. The reason is visible in Table~\ref{tab:breadth-depth}. Regrouping can only improve the depth term, and on the systems where M3 struggles the cost is dominated by breadth, which the remedy does not touch. On H$_2$O stretch it improves M3 by $16\%$ and the better of the two methods by $3\%$.

A second observation from the same experiment bears on where regrouping should act. On H$_4$ side $1.0$ HF the best switch point is the earliest one available: the very first elimination breakpoint, at which sixteen of the twenty-six generators leave at once and ten remain. Regrouping the survivors' union support at that moment needs twelve contexts against the parent grouping's fifty-three. The reason is that the union support collapses disproportionately, and it can be quantified exactly on this case: the sixteen zero-gradient generators account for $708$ of the $1{,}276$ Pauli products in $\mathcal B_0$, or $55.5\%$, and their supports are \emph{disjoint} from those of the ten non-zero generators, whose union is the remaining $568$. Dropping them therefore removes more than half of $\mathcal B_0$ at a stroke.

This is a single case, and the disjointness is a property of the H$_4$ square that should not be assumed elsewhere; the corresponding shares have not been measured on LiH or H$_2$O. Subject to that, it suggests that if regrouping is pursued, the leverage is not at the final pair but as soon as the first substantial group of candidates has been ruled out --- which is also the point at which Part III symmetry filtering would act, without needing to measure anything first.

\section{Finite-shot behaviour}
\label{sec:finite-shot}

Every number above is an oracle planning bound. The elimination rule reads exact
gradients, so it cannot misfire, and the family-wise confidence is asserted rather
than measured. This section replaces the exact gradients with estimates built from
a finite number of shots and runs the same elimination rule on those.

\textbf{Protocol.} A real run does not know the deficits in advance and cannot
jump to the exact breakpoints, so each trial follows a geometric radius schedule:
the common radius starts at $\max_i|g_i|$, wide enough that no arm is eliminated
for free, and is multiplied by $\gamma=0.5$ each round. At each round the
allocation of Eq.~\eqref{eq:allocation} is solved for the current active set and
radius, and each context's shot count is raised to that value if it is higher,
never lowered; the estimate $\widehat g_i$ is then redrawn from
$\mathcal N\!\left(g_i,\ \sum_\alpha\sigma_{i\alpha}^2/n_\alpha\right)$ at the
accumulated $n_\alpha$, which is the distribution of the estimator built from all
shots taken so far. The elimination rule of Eq.~\eqref{eq:bai-elim} is then
applied. A trial ends when one arm remains or the radius falls below
$10^{-3}\max_i|g_i|$. Every case uses 200 trials at seed $0$.

\textbf{The reported inflation mixes two effects and this study does not separate
them.} The schedule is the same coarse $\gamma=0.5$ that Sec.~\ref{sec:sensitivity}
shows overpaying at the planning level, by $1.6$ to $3.8$ across these cases with
no noise at all. So an inflation of, say, $2.2$ is not $2.2$ worth of measurement
noise: an unknown part of it is discretisation. The two cannot be read off against
each other here, because the planning model with $\gamma=0.5$ resolves the leader
to $\mathrm{gap}/2$ while a trial stops as soon as one arm remains, so the
noiseless and noisy runs do not answer the same question. What is unaffected is the
\emph{comparison} between M2 and M3, which use the same schedule, the same initial
radius and the same stopping rule. Separating schedule from noise needs a designed
experiment and is listed in Sec.~\ref{sec:remaining}.

The two deliberate approximations --- the normal limit, and independent
fluctuations per generator --- are stated once with the rest in
Sec.~\ref{sec:assumptions}. The second is exact for the baseline and M2 and an
approximation for M1 and M3; Sec.~\ref{sec:correlation} removes it.

\textbf{Columns.} \emph{Bound} is the oracle planning cost of
Table~\ref{tab:completed}; \emph{Infl.} is the mean total shots over the 200
trials divided by it; \emph{Correct} is the fraction of trials returning the true
maximum-gradient generator. M2/M3 columns are ratios of the corresponding
entries, standard errors combined in quadrature.

\subsection{The one inversion does not survive}

On H$_2$O stretch with the Hartree--Fock reference, the planning bounds give
M2/M3 $=0.86$, the only row in which M3 is more expensive than M2. Under finite
shots the same row gives $1.43\pm0.04$, and M3 wins. The inversion is an artefact
of comparing two bounds that inflate by different amounts, and the conclusion drawn
from it in the previous revision is retracted in Sec.~\ref{sec:conclusions}.

This does not establish the opposite verdict. Sec.~\ref{sec:routines} shows the
same quantity moving over $0.74$ to $1.30$ on the bounds when the grouping routine
is changed, and the finite-shot study was run under one routine only. The
conclusion available here is the weaker one: the bound-level inversion is not
robust, so it cannot be used to claim that M2 beats M3 on that row.

\subsection{The two BAI methods inflate differently}

The inflation is not a common factor. M2 inflates by $2.2$ to $3.2$ on every row.
M3 inflates by $2.3$ to $3.0$ on all four H$_4$ rows, where it tracks M2 closely
and the M2/M3 ratio is preserved to within about $10\%$, but by only $1.40$
to $1.66$ on LiH and both H$_2$O rows, where the M2/M3 ratio roughly doubles. The
separation is far outside the quoted standard errors.

The natural explanation is overshoot. A real run does not know the deficits
$\Delta_i$ in advance, so it discovers them by shrinking a common radius and
overshooting each elimination point. M3's breadth term is a single coarse sweep of
the whole pool, a fixed cost that no schedule can overshoot, whereas every one of
M2's per-generator costs is discovered by overshooting. On that reading the more
breadth-dominated the system, the less of M3's cost is exposed to the schedule.

\textbf{The data support the effect but not that explanation of it.} If
breadth-dominance were the mechanism, the two H$_4$ CISD rows should behave like
LiH and H$_2$O: Table~\ref{tab:breadth-depth} puts their M2 breadth shares at
$87.1\%$ and $57.1\%$, higher than H$_2$O stretch's $80.1\%$ in one case. They do
not. Both show M3 inflating as much as M2, exactly like the depth-dominated H$_4$
HF rows. What the seven rows actually separate on is the system, not the breadth
share: the three large-pool cases behave one way and all four H$_4$ cases the
other. Pool size, context count and breadth share are confounded across four
systems, and the overshoot mechanism remains a hypothesis that a designed
experiment --- the same system at several pool sizes, or the same pool at several
schedules --- has not yet been run to test.

What survives without an explanation is the observation itself: on the three
largest cases here, universal contexts help substantially more under finite shots
than the planning bounds suggest.

\subsection{Reconciling the two directions}
\label{sec:size-trends}

Sec.~\ref{sec:fragmentation} argues that M3's advantage should \emph{decay} as the
system grows; this section finds it \emph{growing}. They are not in contradiction,
because they are statements about different quantities, and it is worth being
explicit about which.

The fragmentation argument is about the \emph{planning bound}, and within it about
the depth term only: as the parent support grows, each individual gradient is
split over more parent contexts, so resolving the final pair through shared
contexts costs more relative to resolving it through their own. That mechanism is
real and is measured in Table~\ref{tab:frag}. The finite-shot result is about
\emph{realised} cost under a schedule, which the bound does not model at all. The
two therefore do not compete, and no mechanism is offered here for the second:
the previous subsection records that the obvious one fails.

Two cautions. The realised M2/M3 ratios that drive the second trend are largest on
H$_2$O ($2.24$ and $1.43$), and conclusion 3 withdraws every M2-versus-M3 verdict
on H$_2$O because the bound-level ratio moves across unity with the grouping
routine; the finite-shot study was run under one routine, so those two entries
inherit the same caveat and should not be used on their own. And ``fading'' in the
conclusions refers only to the bound-level attribution of Sec.~\ref{sec:baseline},
where the sharing-only factor falls from $3.4$ on H$_4$ to $1.1$ on H$_2$O.
Neither trend is established as a function of size: both rest on four systems in
which size, geometry and state vary together.

\subsection{The confidence convention is far stricter than $\delta$, by an amount the data can only bound}
\label{sec:calibration}

No trial in any run selected the wrong generator. Sweeping the family-wise failure
probability over nearly its whole range barely changes that.

\textbf{How much stricter cannot be measured here.} With 300 trials and zero
errors, a Wilson $95\%$ interval puts the true selection-error rate below $1.3\%$
and no lower: the data are consistent with any rate from $0$ to about $1\%$. The figure of $1.2\times10^{-5}$ quoted below is the \emph{analytic
prediction} of Eq.~\eqref{eq:calibration}, not a measurement, and resolving a rate
that small would need on the order of $10^{6}$ trials. What the experiment
establishes is that the realised rate is at most $1.3\%$ where $\delta=0.05$ was
asked for, about four times smaller, and that it barely moves when $\delta$ is
swept over nearly its whole range. That is enough for the practical conclusion ---
$\delta$ is not the knob that controls the selection-error rate --- and not enough
to say by how much the convention over-delivers.

stops when the common radius reaches $\mathrm{gap}/2$, at which point the standard
error of each estimate is $\mathrm{gap}/(2z_\delta)$. The leader's margin over the
runner-up is one gap, so in units of the standard deviation of their difference it
is $2z_\delta/\sqrt2=z_\delta\sqrt2$, and the selection-error probability is
approximately
\begin{equation}
  P(\text{wrong selection})\approx 2\,\overline\Phi\!\left(z_\delta\sqrt2\right),
  \label{eq:calibration}
\end{equation}
where $\overline\Phi$ is the upper-tail standard normal function. This is
$1.6\times10^{-1}$ at $z_\delta=1$, $4.7\times10^{-3}$ at $z_\delta=2$ and
$1.2\times10^{-5}$ at the $z_\delta=3.1$ used in the tables. The displacement
probability for a single competitor is $\overline\Phi(z_\delta\sqrt2)$; the factor
of two is a conservative allowance, not a derivation, covering the fact that the
comparison is between absolute values and that more than one arm may be near the
top. Dropping it would halve every predicted rate and change no conclusion. The
Bonferroni term enters only through $z_\delta$, and the stopping radius already
supplies most of the protection.

with $\delta$, in the following restricted sense. At $\delta=0.05$ and $0.40$ the
prediction is below $10^{-3}$ and the observation is $0/300$, which excludes
$\delta$ itself but does not resolve the prediction. At $\delta=0.90$ and $0.99$
the prediction rises to $2.9\times10^{-3}$ and $3.4\times10^{-3}$, and the
observations, $1$ to $2$ errors in 300, have Wilson intervals of $[0.2,2.4]\%$ and
$[0.1,1.9]\%$, which contain the prediction and exclude $\delta$. The expression is therefore
supported where it is testable and unresolved where it is not.

$z_\delta\approx1.8$ to $2.1$ should be treated as a single-system observation
rather than a recommended default.

\subsection{Good-enough selection}
\label{sec:good-enough}

A run does not have to identify the maximiser; it is often enough to return a
generator that is not much worse. Fix a tolerance $\tau>0$ --- written $\tau$ to
keep it distinct from the deficits $\Delta_i$ --- and stop at the first round $t$
at which every surviving candidate is certified within $\tau$ of the best,
\begin{equation}
  \max_{j\in\mathcal A_t}\left(|\widehat g_j|-r_j\right)
  \ \ge\
  \max_{i\in\mathcal A_t}\left(|\widehat g_i|+r_i\right)-\tau ,
  \label{eq:good-enough}
\end{equation}
returning $\argmax_{i\in\mathcal A_t}|\widehat g_i|$. The run is counted a success
if the returned generator's true absolute gradient is within $\tau$ of the true
maximum.

On H$_4$ side $1.0$ HF at $\tau=\mathrm{gap}/4=5.38\times10^{-3}$, over the same
200 trials at seed $0$ as Table~\ref{tab:finite-shot}, M2 falls from
$1{,}889{,}539\pm64{,}528$ to $1{,}398{,}814\pm32{,}966$ shots and M3 from
$1{,}320{,}830\pm57{,}944$ to $904{,}382\pm20{,}201$, savings of $26\%$ and $31\%$.
In all 200 trials the generator returned was still the exact maximiser. Note that
$\tau=\mathrm{gap}/4$ uses the exact gap, so this inherits the oracle-knowledge
caveat of Sec.~\ref{sec:completed}; a deployment would set $\tau$ from a chemical
accuracy target instead. Measured on one case only.

\section{Parameter sensitivity}
\label{sec:sensitivity}

\subsection{What removing the two free parameters was worth}

The previous revision evaluated M1 at a target radius of $\mathrm{gap}/4$ with no
task-level justification, and evaluated the BAI methods on a geometric schedule
with shrink factor $\gamma=0.5$. Both have been replaced. The M1 target is now
$\mathrm{gap}/2$, the loosest common radius at which a non-adaptive estimate
separates the winner from the field, so that all three methods answer the same
question; this divides the M1 column by four. The BAI methods are evaluated in the
schedule-free limit; a coarse geometric schedule overshoots each elimination point
and overpays.

The schedule change is not cosmetic. Under $\gamma=0.5$, M3 cost more than M1 on
three H$_4$ rows. In the exact limit it does not, on any row
(Sec.~\ref{sec:completed}), so that ordering is a property of the limit rather
than of the method; under a coarse schedule, and under finite shots
(Sec.~\ref{sec:m1-finite}), it can fail.

\subsection{The confidence level does not change the comparisons}

Every planning bound scales with $z_\delta^2$, so every ratio in
Table~\ref{tab:completed} is invariant under the family-wise failure probability.

move by about a factor of two.

realised M2/M3 ratios of $1.60$, $1.36$, $1.77$ and $1.50$ at

with no monotone pattern. What $\delta$ does to the realised error rate is treated
in Sec.~\ref{sec:calibration}.

\section{Within-context correlation}
\label{sec:correlation}

Assumption (iii) of Sec.~\ref{sec:assumptions} is removed here. The covariance of
two generators' fragments inside one context follows from variances alone,
\begin{equation}
  \Cov(F_{i\alpha},F_{j\alpha})
  =\tfrac12\left[\Var(F_{i\alpha}+F_{j\alpha})-\Var(F_{i\alpha})-\Var(F_{j\alpha})\right],
\end{equation}
and sampling from those matrices makes the simulation exact for the shared methods
as well. The matrices are formed per context, symmetrised, and factorised through
an eigendecomposition with negative eigenvalues clipped to zero, which keeps the
draw well defined when a context carries almost no variance.

\textbf{The sampler has been verified.} On all three cases below, every context
covariance is positive semidefinite to rounding (most negative eigenvalue
$-5\times10^{-16}$), every diagonal entry equals the corresponding $\sigma_{i\alpha}^2$
exactly, and the empirical marginal variance of each generator over $2\times10^4$
draws lies between $0.976$ and $1.023$ of its analytic value. The anomaly reported
below is therefore not a marginal-sampling defect.

\subsection{The covariance-aware elimination rule, and a sign defect in it}

Where the \emph{marginal} rule of Eq.~\eqref{eq:bai-elim} compares
$|\widehat g_i|$ and $|\widehat g_j|$ at their individual radii, the
\emph{pairwise} rule compares each pair at the radius of the combination the
comparison actually depends on. Because the comparison is between absolute
values, that combination is $\widehat g_i-s_{ij}\widehat g_j$ with
$s_{ij}=\operatorname{sign}(\widehat g_i\widehat g_j)$, so $i$ is eliminated when
some active $j$ satisfies
\begin{equation}
  |\widehat g_j|-|\widehat g_i| \ > \ z_\delta\sqrt{V_{ij}},
  \qquad
  V_{ij}=\sum_\alpha\frac{1}{n_\alpha}
         \Var\!\left(F_{i\alpha}-s_{ij}F_{j\alpha}\right),
  \label{eq:pairwise}
\end{equation}
with the same $z_\delta$ of Eq.~\eqref{eq:zdelta}. When the two fragments are
correlated in the direction that helps, $V_{ij}$ is below
$\Var(\widehat g_i)+\Var(\widehat g_j)$ and the rule fires earlier.

The sign factor matters and was missing from the previous revision, which used
$\Var(\widehat g_i-\widehat g_j)$ unconditionally. That is not only the wrong
combination for an opposite-sign pair; it is not even well defined, because the
sign of a gradient depends on the arbitrary phase of the Hartree--Fock orbital it
excites from. Only $s_{ij}\Cov(\widehat g_i,\widehat g_j)$ is phase-invariant, and
Eq.~\eqref{eq:pairwise} uses exactly that. The defect is corrected in the numbers
below.

\subsection{Two benign cases and one that is not}

On both H$_4$ side $1.0$ rows, modelling the correlation changes the cost by $1$
to $2\%$, and the covariance-aware rule then cuts M3's realised cost by $40$ to
$43\%$ with no meaningful loss of accuracy. This is the mechanism Part II proposes
for the decisive stage, appearing here as a direct reduction in shots rather than
as the outcome of an optimisation over how to split coefficients.

H$_4$ side $2.0$ HF behaves differently and is \textbf{still not explained}.
Correct selection falls to $76\%$ under the marginal rule and $51\%$ under the
pairwise one, far outside sampling noise, with costs rising more than tenfold. The
sign correction above does not recover it, and neither does correcting the
confidence factor over pairs rather than over generators. Three properties
distinguish this case from the two benign ones, and any explanation has to account
for them:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=2em]
  \item \textbf{The runner-up is exactly degenerate.} \texttt{D 0,3 $\to$ 4,7} and \texttt{D 1,2 $\to$ 5,6} both have $|g|=0.298387$, so there is no unique second arm and $|\mathcal A|=3$.
  \item \textbf{The leading pair is anticorrelated}, with $\Cov=-1.4\times10^{-2}$ against variances of $6.7\times10^{-2}$ and $9.6\times10^{-2}$. The previous revision reported the diagnostic ratio $\Var(\widehat g_1-\widehat g_2)/[\Var(\widehat g_1)+\Var(\widehat g_2)]$ as $0.82$ and concluded that the difference is easier to resolve than independence implies. \emph{That was the wrong quantity}: the ratio so defined is $1.168$ here, and $0.832$ is the value for the sum, which is the relevant combination only because the pair has opposite signs. The corresponding ratios on the two benign rows are $0.929$ and $1.084$. The earlier claim that the obvious explanation is ``contradicted by direct measurement'' is withdrawn.
  \item \textbf{The gap is narrow relative to the field}, $\mathrm{gap}/|g_{(1)}|=5\%$ with $\max_i\Delta_i/\mathrm{gap}=18.9$, so a $17\%$ error in the assumed variance of the decisive comparison has room to matter. H$_4$ side $1.0$ CISD is also anticorrelated, at $1.084$, but its gap is $83\%$ of $|g_{(1)}|$ and nothing goes wrong.
\end{itemize}

A further complication is specific to this system and is recorded in
Sec.~\ref{sec:tests}: the Hartree--Fock orbital phases for square H$_4$ are not
reproducible across SCF starting guesses, so the \emph{relative signs} of the
gradients --- and hence every covariance between two of them --- differ between
runs, while all magnitudes agree. Nothing in the planning bounds or the marginal
rule depends on those signs. The correlated model is the one place they enter.

\textbf{Status.} The independent noise model of Sec.~\ref{sec:finite-shot} remains
the one used for every reported number. The correlated results in
Table~\ref{tab:correlation} should be treated as \emph{unverified} on H$_4$ side
$2.0$ until the anomaly is diagnosed.

\section{Conclusions from the current data}
\label{sec:conclusions}

Table~\ref{tab:evidence} gives the evidence level behind each item. Items marked
there as single-system or bound-only should not be generalised without the work
in Sec.~\ref{sec:remaining}.

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=2em]
  \item The four constructions are implemented as separate modules that save their FC groups, fragment variances and elimination histories, and are evaluated at one criterion with no free schedule or precision parameter. The validation gate passes on all nine cases to $4\times10^{-15}$ Ha.
  \item \textbf{On the planning bounds M3 is cheaper than M1 in every case}, by $1.1$ to $192$ (Table~\ref{tab:completed}). \emph{Observed, not proved} --- the sum-of-maxima accounting of Eq.~\eqref{eq:m3-aggregate} makes the obvious argument incomplete --- and \emph{bound-level only}: on realised cost M3 loses to M1 on both H$_4$ CISD rows (Sec.~\ref{sec:m1-finite}). All M1 columns assume M1 is handed the exact gap.
  \item \textbf{M3 is cheaper than M2 on H$_4$ and LiH; on H$_2$O the comparison is unresolved.} M3 wins eight bound-level rows of nine, but all four H$_2$O rows fall between $0.86$ and $1.13$, inside the $0.74$--$1.30$ range that changing the grouping routine produces. No verdict should be claimed on any H$_2$O row in either direction, including the finite-shot one. The sweep itself covers H$_4$ side $1.0$ HF and LiH only.
  \item \textbf{M2 is not always cheaper than M1}, winning seven bound-level rows of nine and three of seven on realised cost. The counterexamples are the wide-gap rows where static estimation is already cheap; LiH has a comparably wide gap and does not invert, so the gap alone is not the explanation. This comparison is also the most sensitive to M1's oracle gap (App.~\ref{app:m1radius}).
  \item \textbf{The two mechanisms are substitutes.} Their interaction against the no-sharing baseline is below unity in all nine cases, between $0.57$ and $0.96$: elimination removes the situation that sharing exploits.
  \item \textbf{Elimination is the dominant mechanism in seven rows of nine.} Elimination alone is worth $1.27$ to $252.97$ against the baseline, sharing alone $1.11$ to $3.35$; on both H$_4$ CISD rows sharing wins ($2.19$ against $1.27$, $3.11$ against $2.08$). The sharing factor is smallest on all four H$_2$O rows, which with four systems is a correlation with system rather than a trend with size, and refers to planning bounds only.
  \item \textbf{Under finite shots M3's cost inflates less than M2's on the three largest cases.} M2 inflates by $2.2$ to $3.2$ everywhere; M3 by $2.3$ to $3.0$ on the four H$_4$ rows but only $1.40$ to $1.66$ on LiH and both H$_2$O rows, where the M2/M3 ratio roughly doubles (200 trials per row, separations far outside the standard errors). \emph{No mechanism is established}: the breadth-dominance explanation fails on the two H$_4$ CISD rows, and an unseparated part of every inflation figure is discretisation of the $\gamma=0.5$ schedule rather than noise.

Inverting Eq.~\eqref{eq:calibration} for $5\%$ gives $10$ to $12\%$ in simulation, because that expression ignores generators eliminated wrongly earlier in the run. An optimum sits near $z_\delta\approx1.8$--$2.1$ on the one case tested. Invariance of the method ratios to $z_\delta$ holds for the \emph{planning bounds only}: the realised M2/M3 ratio moves over $1.36$ to $1.77$.
  \item \textbf{Conclusions 2 to 6 are routine-independent at bound level on H$_4$ side $1.0$ HF and LiH}, the two rows swept: ratios move by at most $1.45$ and no ordering changes. The sweep covers no other H$_4$ row, no H$_2$O row beyond one parent grouping, and neither the finite-shot nor the correlated-noise study.

\section{Interface with Parts II and III}

Part II improves M3 by using measured variances and covariances to optimise overlapping universal operators and coefficient splitting. Sec.~\ref{sec:breadth-depth} gives that work a sharper target, but Sec.~\ref{sec:overlap} also raises a caution that should be settled before it is built.

The mechanism Part II proposes for the decisive stage is a pairwise-variance identity [AUTHOR: cite Part II]: best-arm identification only requires the pairwise differences to be resolved, and
\begin{equation}
  \Var(\widehat g_i-\widehat g_j)=\sum_\alpha\frac{1}{n_\alpha}\Var\!\left(F_{i\alpha}-F_{j\alpha}\right),
\end{equation}
so the covariance that helps enters through co-measurement inside a shared context, not through the two generators sharing Pauli products. That premise was tested on the finalist pair of each system, inside the parent contexts, by comparing the cost of resolving the difference alone against the cost of resolving both generators to the same radius.

Resolving the difference is cheaper only on H$_4$, by a factor of $1.11$; on LiH and both H$_2$O rows it is more expensive, by factors up to $1.7$. The arithmetic is simple. Writing $S$ for $S^{\text{par}}$ throughout, resolving two generators separately costs $S_i^2+S_j^2$ in units of $\epsilon^{-2}$, while resolving one difference observable costs $S_{i-j}^2$, so the difference wins only when $S_{i-j}<\sqrt{S_i^2+S_j^2}$, which for comparable arms demands roughly $30\%$ cancellation. The measured cancellation, $1-S_{i-j}/(S_i+S_j)$, is $33\%$ on H$_4$ but only $17\%$ on LiH and $7\%$ on H$_2$O stretch. It is tempting to read this against the finalist overlaps of Table~\ref{tab:overlap}, but the two do not line up: H$_4$ has zero finalist overlap and the \emph{largest} cancellation, LiH has $35\%$ overlap and half the cancellation. Sharing Pauli products is evidently not what produces cancellation in the difference --- which is consistent with the premise stated above, that the useful covariance comes from co-measurement inside a context rather than from shared support, but leaves the size of the cancellation unexplained by anything measured here.

This does not refute Part II, whose other mechanisms --- coefficient splitting over an overlapping cover, empirically estimated context covariances, and adaptive choice of the next context to measure --- are untouched by this test. It does mean the pairwise-difference mechanism should not be assumed to pay, and that whether cancellation is available must be measured on the intended benchmark before the variance-aware machinery is designed around it. Sec.~\ref{sec:breadth-depth} adds a second caution: that mechanism acts on the depth term, which is only $12\%$ of the M3 cost on LiH and about $39\%$ on H$_2$O stretch, so its total effect is bounded even where it does pay.

Part III changes the ADAPT trajectory so that the commutators first become symmetry-filtered and tapered. When Part III is active, the Part I object $C_i$ is replaced by
\begin{equation}
  C_{i,\mathrm{tap}}^{\parallel,k}
  =\mathrm{Taper}_{\mathcal R_k}\left(\Pi_{\mathcal R_k}([H,G_i])\right),
\end{equation}
and then M1, M2, or M3 is applied to these reduced operators. Part I tables do not include this reduction.

\section{Remaining work in Part I}
\label{sec:remaining}

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=2em]
  \item \textbf{Diagnose the H$_4$ side $2.0$ correlated-noise failure} (Sec.~\ref{sec:correlation}). The sampler's marginals and positive semidefiniteness are verified and a sign defect in the pairwise rule is fixed, so neither explains it. The next candidates are the exactly degenerate runner-up, which leaves no unique second arm, and the interaction of the anticorrelated leading pair with the elimination threshold. Until this is settled the correlated model cannot be used for reported numbers.
  \item \textbf{Extend the routine sweep to the remaining H$_2$O rows.} One row has been swept and it dissolved a conclusion. The other three have not, nor have the individual groupings that M2 and the baseline depend on, nor the finite-shot and correlated-noise studies, which were run under one routine throughout. This is the single largest gap in the evidence base.
  \item \textbf{Test the confidence convention beyond one case.} The optimum at $z_\delta\approx1.8$ to $2.1$ rests on H$_4$ side $1.0$ HF alone. It should be measured on LiH and at least one H$_2$O row before any convention is adopted, because choosing it changes every absolute number and all tables would have to be regenerated.
  \item \textbf{Test good-enough selection beyond one case} before making it the default stopping rule. The $30\%$ saving is measured on H$_4$ side $1.0$ HF only, and the tolerance used, $\mathrm{gap}/4$, is derived from the exact gap; a deployable version needs a tolerance defined from a chemical accuracy target instead.
  \item \textbf{Simulate the two H$_2$O CISD rows}, the only cases missing from Table~\ref{tab:finite-shot}. They are the rows where the planning bound is largest, so they are the ones where a realised-versus-bound discrepancy would matter most.
  \item \textbf{Separate schedule discretisation from measurement noise} in the finite-shot inflation. The $\gamma=0.5$ schedule overpays by $1.6$ to $3.8$ at the planning level with no noise at all, so the reported inflations of $1.4$ to $3.2$ are a mixture of the two that this study cannot decompose, the noiseless and noisy runs having different stopping rules. Running the surrogate across several $\gamma$ would settle it and would also show how much of the reported cost a tuned schedule recovers.
  \item \textbf{Measure the excluded cost components.} The shot metric prices neither circuit depth nor the number of distinct measurement settings, and the latter differs by $3.5$ to $6.1$ times between the method families (Table~\ref{tab:settings}). Until that is priced, the M2-versus-M3 comparison is incomplete even where the shot counts are resolved.
  \item \textbf{Complete the test suite.} Sec.~\ref{sec:tests} lists what is covered and what is not; the highest-value gaps are the brute-force commutator and variance checks, an adversarial search for a counterexample to $M_{\mathrm{M3}}\le M_{\mathrm{M1}}$, and a shot-level sampling check of the Gaussian surrogate.
\end{enumerate}

\section{What is tested, and what is not}
\label{sec:tests}

\textbf{Covered.} The suite of 26 unit tests pins the conventions that were
previously wrong and the pieces with closed forms: the computational-basis
ordering and big-endian convention; the qubit Hamiltonian against the PySCF RHF
energy on the Hartree--Fock determinant and against the PySCF FCI energy in the
target sector, on every case, as a hard gate; the top gradient against a
finite-difference energy derivative; the context-shot allocation against
Eq.~\eqref{eq:single-gradient-shot} for a single gradient; and the fragment
decomposition summing back to the gradient operator. Alongside these,
\texttt{verify\_part1.py} runs 68 checks on the published results: that each
reported total is the sum of the file that claims to explain it, that the
validation gate passed, that the allocation meets its confidence target, that
$M_{\mathrm{M3}}\le M_{\mathrm{M1}}$, that all methods agree on the selected
generator, that repeated runs agree exactly, that the ratios are invariant to
$\delta$, and that a geometric radius schedule converges onto the exact limit.
All pass.

\textbf{Not covered.} The following would materially raise confidence and have not
been done. They are listed in rough order of value.

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=2em]
  \item \textbf{Brute-force commutators and variances.} Every $C_i=\comm{H}{G_i}$ should be checked against a dense matrix commutator on H$_4$, every gradient (not only the top one) against finite differences, and every fragment variance against a dense $\expect{F^2}-\expect{F}^2$. The pipeline is currently validated at its ends but not term by term.
  \item \textbf{Grouping validity.} That every pair within a context commutes, that the contexts partition $\mathcal B_0$ exactly once, and that each restricted context $\mathcal M_\alpha\cap\mathcal B_t$ is still fully commuting, are asserted by construction rather than tested.
  \item \textbf{Shot-level sampling of the joint distribution.} The per-context covariances and the marginals they generate are now checked (Sec.~\ref{sec:correlation}), but the joint draw has not been compared against sampling actual measurement outcomes, which is what would settle the H$_4$ side $2.0$ anomaly.
  \item \textbf{$M_{\mathrm{M3}}\le M_{\mathrm{M1}}$ adversarially.} The ordering is checked on nine cases and argued informally; random multi-constraint instances should be searched for a counterexample to the sum-of-maxima aggregation, since finding one would turn conclusion 2 from an observation into a bounded claim.
  \item \textbf{The Gaussian surrogate against shot-level sampling.} On H$_4$, contexts could be diagonalised and bitstrings sampled, and the empirical mean and variance of $\widehat g_i$ compared with the normal model. Assumption (i) of Sec.~\ref{sec:assumptions} is currently untested.
  \item \textbf{Allocation optimality.} The fixed-point solver agrees with a general convex solver on the small instances tried; the KKT residual should be reported as a matter of course, and multi-constraint instances tested systematically.
  \item \textbf{Analytically known answers.} H$_2$ in a minimal basis has gradients, commutators and fragment variances obtainable by hand; a two-arm synthetic BAI problem with a known gap would pin the elimination order and Eq.~\eqref{eq:m2-exact}.
  \item \textbf{Limiting cases and symmetry.} A single-generator pool must give $M_1=M_2=M_3$; a vanishing gap must diverge as $1/\mathrm{gap}^2$; gradients that vanish by symmetry must fall below threshold; results must be invariant under relabelling qubits or permuting the pool; the pool sizes $26$, $92$, $140$ should be checked against the combinatorial formula and every generator against anti-Hermiticity.
  \item \textbf{Regression pinning.} Every table entry should be pinned to a stored value with a commit hash, so that any change is caught rather than discovered.
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{One reproducibility limit is known and not a test gap.} The Hartree--Fock
orbitals of square H$_4$ are determined only up to the sign of each molecular
orbital, and different SCF starting guesses return different signs. Gradient
\emph{magnitudes}, fragment variances and every quantity in the planning bounds
are unaffected, and repeated runs from the same starting guess agree exactly. The
relative \emph{signs} of two gradients, and hence the covariance between them, are
not reproducible unless the phases are fixed. Only the correlated noise model of
Sec.~\ref{sec:correlation} depends on them. Fixing a phase convention --- for
instance, requiring the largest-magnitude coefficient of each orbital to be
positive --- would remove this, and should be done before that model is used.

The implementation should be regarded as validated at the level these tests reach
and no further. Two defects of the previous revision were silent and were caught
only by end-to-end energy comparison (App.~\ref{app:status}); the list above is
written in that light.

\section{Revision history and relationship to the previous revision}
\label{app:revision}

\textbf{Note on the finite-shot tables.} The finite-shot study was regenerated for this revision: every row now uses 200 trials at seed $0$, where the H$_2$O rows previously used 40, and standard errors and Wilson intervals are reported throughout. Totals therefore differ from the previous revision at the few-per-cent level of the run-to-run scatter (for instance LiH M3, $882{,}018$ before and $893{,}509\pm19{,}804$ now). The correlated-noise table was also regenerated after the sign correction described in Sec.~\ref{sec:correlation}. The breadth metric of Table~\ref{tab:breadth-depth} changed definition: it was previously the share paid by the top two arms for M2, which gave $53.8\%$ on H$_4$ side $1.0$ HF against $3.0\%$ under Eq.~\eqref{eq:breadth-depth}.

\textbf{What this revision changed.} All numbers are produced by a single
from-geometry implementation in which each method is a separate module that saves
the fully commuting groups and the fragment variances behind every table entry.
Every run passes the validation gate of Sec.~\ref{sec:computational}. All nine
cases are complete, H$_2$O included. Both free parameters of the previous revision
have been removed: M1 is evaluated at the target radius that matches the
winner-identification task, and the BAI methods in the schedule-free exact limit
rather than on a geometric radius schedule. Two further pieces were added: the
no-sharing baseline of Sec.~\ref{sec:baseline}, which completes the two-by-two the
methods span, and the finite-shot surrogate of Sec.~\ref{sec:finite-shot}, which
retracts one conclusion of the earlier revision and shows the confidence
convention to be far stricter than the stated failure probability requires.


The physics of the previous revision is confirmed. Universal Pauli supports, gradient values, gaps and selected generators are reproduced to six or more digits. The present code was written from the molecular geometry upward rather than derived from the previous revision's scripts, and shares no module with them; it is \emph{not} an independent reimplementation in the stronger sense, since it was written by the same project against the same conventions, so the agreement is evidence that the physics layer is correct and not that two independent groups agree. What changed is the cost model, for three reasons.

First, the fully commuting grouping. All rows now use one deterministic greedy routine; the previous revision's H$_4$ and H$_2$O rows used a different partitioning, giving 356 and 9,874 contexts where greedy gives 53 and 2,366. Cost grows with the number of fragments an operator is split across, so this alone is a factor of several.

Second, the previous M1 column was not a single model. For LiH it is reproduced within 8\% by a uniform per-context allocation at one standard error sized to a \emph{typical} rather than worst-case generator (1,929,181 against the reported 2,088,380), which guarantees the target precision for about half the gradient vector rather than all of it. For H$_4$ no combination of grouping, allocation and confidence convention reproduces the reported 182,282,619: uniform allocation at worst-case variance with the previous revision's own 356 contexts lands near $3\times10^7$. That entry also exceeds its own no-sharing baseline of 11,326,894 by a factor of 16. That is not strictly impossible --- a shared grouping fragments each gradient, so with a fragmentation penalty $f_i>1$ an allocation over shared contexts can in principle cost more than measuring each generator in its own grouping, and a bound of the form $\max_i f_i$ times the baseline is the most that can be claimed in general. But the $f_i$ measured here are between $1.07$ and $3.62$ (Table~\ref{tab:frag}), and the present M1 is \emph{below} its baseline on all nine rows, so a factor of 16 above it remains unexplained by any mechanism in the model.

Third, the allocation itself. On LiH, solving Eq.~\eqref{eq:allocation} costs about 17 times fewer shots than spreading the same total uniformly over the 980 parent contexts, at the same one-standard-error target and the same $z_\delta=1$. At one standard error the present M1 is \emph{cheaper} than the previously reported value, 1,191,917 against 2,088,380, despite covering the worst-case generator rather than a typical one. The larger absolute values in Table~\ref{tab:completed} come principally from applying a Bonferroni family-wise $z$ over the pool, a conservative convention applied identically to all three methods and therefore invisible in the ratios.

The consequence is that the previous M1/M2 ratios, which ranged from 0.28 to 627, should not be used. They combine an optimistic LiH entry with an unexplained H$_4$ entry.

\section{Implementation status and past defects}
\label{app:status}

The following items of the previous revision's cleanup list are complete.
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=2em]
  \item Separate output files named with the method codes \texttt{M1\_FCUG}, \texttt{M2\_BAIFCIG} and \texttt{M3\_BAIFCUG}.
  \item FC groups, fragment standard deviations and BAI elimination histories saved for every number in every table.

Two implementation defects of the previous revision are worth recording, because both were silent. The two-electron integrals were inserted into the spin-orbital tensor in chemist rather than physicist ordering, with the spin pattern $(a,b,a,b)$ instead of $(a,b,b,a)$ and without the one-half prefactor; and the computational basis was indexed little-endian where the sparse-operator convention is big-endian. For H$_4$ the resulting Hartree--Fock energy was $-5.21$ Ha against an RHF value of $-1.76$ Ha. The validation gate described in Sec.~\ref{sec:computational} exists to make a repeat of this impossible.
